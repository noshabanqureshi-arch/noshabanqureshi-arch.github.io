Four useful content pillars are usually enough.

A content pillar should make decisions easier. If your categories are so vague that almost anything fits—or so narrow that you run out of ideas after a week—they are not doing their job.

Start with what the customer needs to understand.

Formats like Reels, carousels, quotes and behind-the-scenes posts are not really content pillars. They describe how you present an idea. A useful pillar describes what the business repeatedly needs to communicate.

Pillar 1

The problem you solve

Questions, symptoms, mistakes, myths, consequences and useful beginner education around the customer problem.

Pillar 2

Your process or method

How you think, what steps matter, what decisions you make, what good work looks like and what happens behind the scenes.

Pillar 3

Questions + objections

Pricing questions, common hesitations, comparisons, misconceptions, timelines, expectations and “what happens if…” questions.

Pillar 4

Your offer + next steps

What you sell, who it is for, use cases, inclusions, how to start and what someone should do when they are ready.

Where do stories and values go?

You do not necessarily need a separate “personal” or “behind the scenes” pillar. Relevant stories, values and points of view can sit inside the four pillars.

For example, a story about why you changed part of your process belongs naturally under the process pillar. A story about a customer misconception you see all the time may belong under objections. A founder value that changes how you deliver the service can support the offer or process pillar.

Use the repeatability test.

A good pillar should pass three questions:
  1. Can I think of at least 10 useful angles inside this topic?
  2. Does the topic help a potential customer understand something important about the problem, process or offer?
  3. Could I return to this pillar next month with a different question, example or format?

One pillar can create many formats.

Suppose your pillar is customer questions and objections. One real question can become:

  • a Reel answering it in 30 seconds;
  • a carousel showing the answer step by step;
  • a Story poll about the misconception behind it;
  • a longer resource article;
  • an email answering the part people misunderstand most;
  • an FAQ on the sales page.

That is why pillars reduce workload: you are not inventing unrelated content. You are reusing a meaningful business theme.

Do not force equal volume.

Your four pillars do not need exactly the same number of posts. The mix should reflect what the business needs that month.

If you are launching an offer, the offer and objection pillars may need more attention. If the audience does not yet understand the problem, educational content may need more room. The pillar system gives structure without turning the calendar into a rigid quota.

A quick example

For a local accountant, four pillars could become:

  • Problem: confusing bookkeeping and tax mistakes;
  • Process: how records are organized and what good monthly bookkeeping looks like;
  • Questions: deadlines, deductible expenses, when to incorporate, what records to keep;
  • Offer: monthly bookkeeping, tax preparation and who each service is for.

The same framework works for a consultant, clinic, photographer, home-service company or other expertise-led business because the pillar is tied to the customer journey rather than a specific social platform.
